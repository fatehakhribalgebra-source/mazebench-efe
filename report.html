Technical note · Active inference on a sparse grid benchmark

Playing MazeBench by minimising expected free energy

An agent with no reward function: it imagines short futures in the latent space of a hierarchical world model and takes the action whose futures are least surprising and most informative.

9 November 2026 ~6,000 lines Python · 47 tests Status: pre-training

1 What the benchmark asks for

MazeBench is a 16 × 16 grid of hand-built rooms, each itself 16 × 16 tiles, played through an ASCII view and eight keys. Gems are the score. The agent sees one room at a time and is told nothing about where it is in the world.

Three properties make it hard in a way that defeats ordinary reinforcement learning. The reward is almost absent: 90 gems exist across 256 rooms, so a random walk of a few hundred steps collects nothing and learns nothing from it. The glyph alphabet is permuted between runs, so a wall is not reliably #. And the published win condition asks for 100 gems from a world that contains 90 — consistent with the best reported scores sitting around 10 %.

With no usable reward gradient, the agent needs an objective that rewards finding out as well as getting. That objective is expected free energy.

2 The loop

3 The objective

Expected free energy scores a candidate plan rather than a state. It asks: if I followed this plan, how far would the futures I expect sit from the futures I prefer, how uncertain would my observations be, and how much would I learn? The agent takes the first action of the plan that scores lowest, then replans.

4 The world model

Planning needs a model to plan in, and pixel or glyph prediction is the wrong target: a grid world is mostly static, so a predictor that outputs "nothing moved" scores about 99 % on raw tiles while being useless. The model is therefore a JEPA — it predicts in representation space, and is scored only on the tiles that actually changed.

The encoder maps the egocentric window to z of shape (64, 31, 31): one feature vector per tile. The window is 31 wide because the engine's camera frames a whole 16-tile room, and 2·16−1 is what it takes to cover a room from any corner. Collapse is prevented the usual way, with VICReg variance and covariance terms against an EMA target encoder; on the trained checkpoint latent variance sits at 0.15, well clear of zero.

On top of the primitive level sit one, two or three abstract levels — a fixed stack of 2, 3 or 4 in total, as in H-JEPA, not an arbitrary depth. Each level consumes chunks of four states from the level below, so strides multiply geometrically and the planner's effective lookahead grows with depth while the number of rollout steps it pays for stays at eight.

5 Imagination, and the box on the gem

The planner is a cross-entropy method over discrete action sequences: sample 256 sequences of 8 actions from a per-step categorical, roll each one forward through the latent predictor, score it with G, refit the categorical on the best 32, repeat four times. Nothing touches the engine — the whole search happens on imagined latents, and the previous plan is carried forward as a prior so the agent commits to a direction instead of dithering.

Because the rollouts are imagined, the agent can be rewarded for configurations it has never observed. The box-layout term hashes the pushable-block mask with a Zobrist key, so a plan that ends with a block somewhere no block has ever been scores as novel — including the case of a block pushed onto a gem.

6 What is measured, and against what

An exploration claim is only as good as its control, so the same agent runs in four configurations on shared seeds. Full is everything. No-epistemic deletes the information-gain term, leaving a planner that only chases preferred outcomes. Memoryless keeps the term but wipes the visit counts, so novelty is undefined. Random is a uniform action policy. If the full arm does not separate from these, the free-energy machinery is decoration. The run below exercised three of the four; the no-epistemic arm is implemented but waits on the trained model, since without a working gem head a purely pragmatic planner has nothing to pursue.

7 Where training stands

One model has been trained end to end, on a deliberately small buffer, to check that the losses move in the right direction before the real run. They do, for the parts with data behind them.

That diagnosis is what the overnight collection was for. A coverage policy — breadth-first search over the rendered board, with a blocked-move set and 22 % random actions to get camera rotations into the buffer — was run from the 86 gem-bearing rooms.

8 What this is not yet

Open, and load-bearing

  • The engine has never been run from this project. Level geometry, gem and block placement are parsed from the real level files, and room adjacency comes from the shipped world map — but the dynamics stepping those rooms are a reimplementation. Every number above is mock dynamics on real geometry, and none of them is a MazeBench score.
  • Elevation is approximated. A stacked # column is ground at that height with a one-step climb rule, which is enough to keep the levels traversable; ice, slopes, lifts, buttons and moving hazards are not modelled.
  • No gems in the ablation. The benchmark's start room contains none, so the ladder in Figure 5 compares exploration only. A gem-collecting comparison needs the trained model and gem-bearing starts.
  • The transfer path is untested. Frozen V-JEPA 2 and SigLIP 2 encoders are wired in behind an adapter at pinned revisions, but no real frame has passed through them — the ASCII view is what the benchmark gives, and pixels would need a browser in the loop.
